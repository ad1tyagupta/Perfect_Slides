# Teaching and Tutorials

Lectures, workshops, training, onboarding, explainers. The audience is learning
something new and will come back to these slides afterwards.

This is the only deck type built to be read twice.

---

## The shape

| Beat | Carries |
|---|---|
| **Why this matters** | What they can do afterwards that they cannot do now |
| **The map** | What is coming, so they can place each part |
| **One concept at a time** | Explain, show, then apply |
| **Checkpoints** | A question or exercise every few concepts |
| **Recap** | The map again, with everything filled in |
| **Where next** | Resources, practice, further reading |

Fifteen to twenty-five slides per hour of teaching.

---

## Explain, show, apply

The pattern for every concept. Skipping any step is why people nod along and
retain nothing.

1. **Explain.** The idea in plain words, before any jargon.
2. **Show.** A worked example, concrete and specific.
3. **Apply.** A question they answer, or a variation they work out.

The third step is the one usually cut for time and it is the one that makes the
learning stick.

---

## Define terms once, then use them

Introduce a term deliberately, on its own, with a plain-language definition.
Then use it consistently for the rest of the deck.

Never introduce three terms on one slide. Never use a term before defining it.
Never switch between two words for the same thing.

A glossary slide near the end is worth the space in any deck with more than five
new terms.

---

## Build diagrams up

A complete diagram appearing at once is a wall. The audience spends the next
thirty seconds decoding it instead of listening.

Build it in stages across consecutive slides, adding one part at a time, with the
new part in the accent colour and the earlier parts muted. By the final slide the
whole picture is there and they watched it assemble.

This is worth the extra slides. Slides are free.

---

## Written to be revisited

Unlike a conference talk, these slides are read again later without you.

- Enough context on each slide to make sense alone
- Speaker notes carry the spoken detail, and get exported with the deck
- Medium density: fuller than a talk, lighter than a report
- Number every slide so people can ask about slide 14

Test: if someone who missed the session read only the slides, would they get most
of it? For teaching, the answer should be yes.

---

## Checkpoints

Every four or five concepts, a slide that asks something.

A question with a pause. A short exercise. A "what would happen if" with the
answer on the next slide. It breaks the passive stretch where attention drifts
and tells you whether anyone is following.

Put the answer on the following slide, not the same one.

---

## Voice

Patient and plain. Short sentences. No cleverness.

Assume intelligence, not knowledge. The audience is capable and does not yet know
this thing. Talking down is as bad as going too fast.

Never say "simply", "just", or "obviously". If it were obvious they would not be
here, and the word makes anyone who is struggling feel stupid.

---

## Design

Calm and consistent. The same layout for the same kind of content every time, so
structure becomes predictable and the audience spends attention on the content
rather than on navigating.

Use `Blueprint` or `Clinic` from the presets. Light background prints well and
survives a bad projector.

---

## Common failures

- **Jargon before definitions.**
- **A finished diagram appearing all at once.**
- **No practice.** Explaining and showing without applying feels complete and is not.
- **Slides that only work with narration**, when people will read them later.
- **"Simply" and "just".**
- **Too much per slide** because slides feel expensive. They are not. Split.
