# Conference Talks

For a stage, a room, and a speaker. The slides are not the talk. They support a
person who is already saying the words.

The most common mistake is building a document and reading it aloud.

---

## The shape

Talks are not structured like documents. They run on tension and release.

| Beat | Carries |
|---|---|
| **The hook** | A question, a surprising fact, a story opening. First thirty seconds |
| **The stakes** | Why this matters to the people in the room |
| **The journey** | Three to five movements, each with its own small tension |
| **The turn** | The moment the audience sees it differently |
| **The payoff** | What to do with this |
| **The close** | One line they repeat afterwards |

Fifteen to thirty slides for a twenty-minute talk. Slides are cheap. Advance them
often.

---

## Low density, always

One idea per slide. Often one word, one number, or one image.

If the audience is reading, they are not listening. Anything they have to read
competes with the person speaking, and the person loses.

| On the slide | In the speaker notes |
|---|---|
| The number | Where it came from and what it means |
| The image | The story behind it |
| Three words | The paragraph |

Everything cut from a slide goes into the notes. Presenter view (`P`) shows them
alongside a timer and the next slide.

---

## Type is big

Someone is sitting at the back of a room the size of a cinema.

- Statement slides: 90 to 140px at stage size
- Nothing below 28px, ever
- Two to three lines maximum per slide
- High contrast, and assume the projector is bad

---

## The turn

Every talk worth giving has one moment where the audience sees something
differently. Find it and build toward it.

Give it its own slide, usually a statement slide with nothing else on it. Slow
down. Let it sit.

If there is no turn, the talk is a report. Reports are better as documents.

---

## Images over diagrams

A photograph that carries the feeling of the point beats a diagram explaining it.
Full bleed, no border, no caption. Speak over it.

Save diagrams for the two or three places where the structure genuinely is the
point.

---

## Voice

Written to be spoken. Fragments are fine. Repetition for rhythm is fine here, and
only here, because spoken emphasis works differently from written emphasis.

Read every line aloud. If it does not sound like you talking, rewrite it.

---

## Practical

- **Number the slides** so people can reference them in questions.
- **A closing slide with your name and where to find you**, left up during Q and A.
- **Never say "as you can see on this slide".** If they can see it, say the point.
- **Assume no internet.** Everything embedded, nothing loaded live.
- **Assume 4:3 is possible.** Some venues still have it. Keep important content
  away from the far left and right edges.

---

## Common failures

- **Paragraphs on slides.** The audience reads instead of listening.
- **Reading the slides aloud.** Then the slides were the talk and you were not needed.
- **No hook.** The first minute decides whether they stay with you.
- **A dense diagram on stage.** Nobody parses it in the twelve seconds you give it.
- **Ending on questions.** End on the line you want repeated, then take questions.
