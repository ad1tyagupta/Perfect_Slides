# Research Presentations

Presenting findings from a study: market research, user research, an academic
project, a data analysis. The audience needs to believe the finding, which means
they need to believe the method.

**This is the opposite shape from a consulting deck.** Consulting leads with the
answer because the client hired you for judgement. Research earns the answer,
because an unexamined finding is just an opinion with a chart.

**Density: 40 to 70 words a slide**, rising to 100 or more on method and data
slides where the detail is the point.

---

## The sequence

Twelve to eighteen slides.

| # | Slide | Carries |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Title** | Study name, who ran it, when |
| 2 | **Background** | Why this study exists |
| 3 | **Research question** | What exactly you set out to answer |
| 4 | **Objectives** | What answering it makes possible |
| 5 | **Method** | What you did, and why that method |
| 6 | **Sample** | Who or what, how many, how selected |
| 7 | **Data** | What you collected, shown honestly |
| 8 | **Key insights** | Three to five findings, one slide each |
| 9 | **Trends and patterns** | What moves together, what changed |
| 10 | **Competitive or comparative view** | How this sits against alternatives |
| 11 | **Opportunities** | What the findings make possible |
| 12 | **Limitations** | What this study cannot tell you |
| 13 | **Recommendations** | What to do about it |
| 14 | **Conclusion and next steps** | The answer, restated, and what follows |

An executive summary after the title is fine when the deck will be circulated.
It does not replace the method section, it just lets a busy reader stop early.

---

## Method before findings

The rule that defines the type. State what you did before you say what you found.

Someone who disagrees with your conclusion will attack the method. Getting there
first, plainly and without defensiveness, is what makes the finding survive.

The method slide answers four things: what you did, on whom, over what period,
and why that approach rather than an obvious alternative.

> 412 checkout sessions recorded across four weeks, March to April 2026.
> Recruited from active customers only, so this says nothing about people who
> never signed up. Session recording rather than survey, because we wanted
> observed behaviour rather than reported behaviour.

That last clause is the one people skip and the one that earns trust.

---

## Sample size and selection go on the slide

Not in an appendix. A finding from 12 people and a finding from 1,200 are
different kinds of statement, and the audience needs to know which they are
hearing while they are hearing it.

Say how people were selected, not just how many. A self-selected sample of 1,200
is often weaker than a randomised 200, and pretending otherwise is the most
common quiet dishonesty in research decks.

---

## One insight per slide

Findings are the reason the deck exists. Give each one room.

Each insight slide carries: the finding as the headline, the evidence as a
chart, and one line on what it means. Then stop.

Three to five insights. If you have eleven, you have a report and the deck
should carry the five that change a decision.

---

## Limitations are not an apology

The slide that separates credible research from advocacy.

Say what the study cannot tell you. Where the data was thin. What you would do
differently. Which conclusions are firm and which are directional.

Presenting limitations *raises* confidence in everything else, because it shows
you know the difference between what you measured and what you inferred.

> This is behaviour on desktop only. Mobile was excluded for recording reasons,
> and mobile is 60% of traffic, so treat the size of the effect as unproven.

---

## Show the data honestly

- Axes start at zero unless there is a stated reason, and the reason goes on the slide
- Show the spread, not only the average. A mean hides everything interesting
- Small samples get their n printed on the chart
- Distinguish what you measured from what you modelled, visually
- Contradictory data gets shown, not smoothed

If the data is ambiguous, the slide should look ambiguous. A clean chart over
messy data is a lie told with design.

---

## Sourcing

Every external figure carries a source line. Own data says so and names the
period and sample. Anything modelled is labelled as modelled, with the
assumptions in an appendix.

## Voice

Precise and unhurried. Hedge where hedging is honest, which is a difference from
every other deck type here. "Suggests" and "is consistent with" are correct when
that is what the data supports. Overclaiming is the failure mode.

Still no filler, and still no repetition.

---

## Common failures

- **Findings before method.** The audience cannot weigh a number they do not
  know the origin of.
- **Sample size hidden in an appendix.**
- **No limitations slide.** Reads as advocacy.
- **Every chart the same size**, so nothing signals which finding matters.
- **Recommendations that outrun the data.** A study of 40 people does not
  support a company-wide restructure.
- **Truncated axes without saying so.**
