# Pitch Decks

For raising money, or selling to someone with a budget. They have seen forty
decks this month and will give yours ninety seconds before deciding whether to
pay attention.

**Density: 20 to 40 words a slide.** Keynote register. One idea per slide, large
type, heavy white space. If a slide holds two arguments it is two slides.

---

## The sequence

Fourteen beats, broadly the structure Sequoia recommends. Twelve to eighteen
slides, since some beats take two.

| # | Slide | Carries |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Title and one-line idea** | Company, and what you do in plain words |
| 2 | **Problem** | Who hurts, and how much. Named people, not a market |
| 3 | **Solution** | What you built, in one sentence |
| 4 | **Product or demo** | Show it. Screens, not description |
| 5 | **Why now** | What changed that makes this possible today |
| 6 | **Target market and size** | Built bottom up |
| 7 | **Business model** | How money is made, per unit |
| 8 | **Go to market** | How you reach customers, and what it costs |
| 9 | **Traction** | Proof. The most important slide in the deck |
| 10 | **Competition and advantage** | Honest positioning, real competitors |
| 11 | **Team** | Why you specifically |
| 12 | **Financial projections** | Three years, with the assumptions visible |
| 13 | **Funding ask and use of funds** | How much, what for, what it buys |
| 14 | **Closing vision** | What this becomes if it works |

---

## Adjust for stage

The sequence is a spine, not a checklist. Cutting the wrong slides looks
evasive, cutting the right ones looks focused.

| Stage | Drop or shrink | Emphasise |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-seed** | Financial projections to one line. Go to market stays a hypothesis | Problem, why now, team |
| **Seed** | Projections to a single chart | Traction, product, why now |
| **Series A** | Nothing | Traction, unit economics, go to market |
| **Series B onward** | Problem and solution compress to one slide each | Financials, market position, efficiency |

At pre-seed, a detailed five-year model reads as fiction and costs credibility.
At Series A, its absence reads as unpreparedness.

---

## Traction outranks everything

If you have real numbers they move earlier, sometimes straight after the
problem. A pitch with traction is a different conversation from one without.

Show the shape of growth, not one number. Six months of a chart beats a single
figure, because the direction is what they are buying.

No traction yet? Say so plainly and lead on the insight in "why now" instead.
Investors forgive early. They do not forgive vagueness that reads as hiding.

---

## The two slides most decks get wrong

**Why now.** Everyone can see the problem. Why has nobody solved it, and what
changed recently that makes this possible? A technology got cheap, a regulation
moved, a behaviour shifted. Without an answer, the honest question is why this
did not already exist.

> ✗ The market for X is large and growing
> ✓ Everyone builds this for enterprises. The buyers are actually two-person
>   teams, and since the API opened last year they can be reached directly.

**Use of funds.** Not a pie chart of departments. Say what the money buys and
what it proves. "Eighteen months to 10,000 paying users, which is what a Series
A needs to see."

## Rules

**Numbers, never adjectives.** Not "strong growth" but "400 to 1,900 users in
five months". Every adjective on a pitch slide is a number you did not have.

**Build the market bottom up.** This many customers, paying this much, is this.
Top-down sizing from an industry report is the fastest way to look inexperienced.

**Name real competitors.** Claiming none says you have not looked. Position
honestly and say what you trade away.

**State the ask plainly.** How much, for what, what it achieves. Not "we are
exploring opportunities".

**No appendix in the main deck.** Detail lives after the ask, for the questions
you hope to get.

---

## Voice

Confident and specific. No hedging, no "we believe we may be able to". If you
are unsure, leave it out rather than qualifying it into noise.

Contractions are fine. It should sound like someone who knows their business.

---

## Common failures

- **Fifteen slides before the product appears.** Show it by slide four.
- **Adjectives standing in for traction.** "Rapid growth" means no numbers.
- **Top-down market sizing.** One percent of a huge market is not a plan.
- **Claiming no competition.** Reads as not having checked.
- **A vague ask.** If the number is not on a slide, nobody remembers it.
- **The team slide as a row of past employer logos.** Say why these people, for
  this problem.
- **Projections with invisible assumptions.** The assumptions are the argument.
